Letting an agent act while you sleep

ALFRED is a local-first life-optimisation system: it plans my week, watches what actually happens, and adapts — over Discord, on my hardware, with no cloud in the loop. It is also the only agent in this studio allowed to act on its own schedule. This is the safety envelope that makes that tolerable, and what building it taught me about autonomy's real price.

Every other agent in the studio's structure acts when spoken to: the coding fleet works a prompt, the cron bots run a fixed script and touch nothing but their own JSON. ALFRED is different in kind. Its Conductor decomposes goals into weekly plans, reconciles them against real capacity, sends messages, and reschedules — unprompted. The moment software crosses from "answers me" to "acts for me", the design question inverts. It stops being "what can it do?" and becomes "what happens when it's wrong?" — because at some point, unattended, it will be.

Local-first is a safety property

ALFRED runs on my own hardware, on local models via Ollama, with SQLite as its memory. That's usually pitched as privacy, and it is — your goals, habits and failures are the most personal dataset you own. But it's also containment: the blast radius of a misbehaving agent is bounded by the machine it lives on and the credentials it holds. A local agent with no cloud writes can embarrass itself; it cannot surprise you with a bill, leak a life's telemetry, or act somewhere you can't see. Constraint one costs nothing and buys the most.

The envelope: three gates on every tool call

Inside the process, nothing the model "decides" is trusted directly. Every tool call passes through the same three gates:

  1. An allowlist. The set of callable tools is closed and enumerated. There is no "the model found a clever way to..." — capability that isn't on the list doesn't exist, no matter how persuasive the plan that wanted it.
  2. A capability tier. Tools are ranked by consequence: reading state is cheap, writing plans costs more, messaging a human or touching the schedule sits at the top. Higher tiers face stricter checks — the agent's authority is graduated, not binary. The Conductor earns its tier-3 autonomy; it isn't granted by default to everything in the process.
  3. An audit trail. Every call is logged with its arguments and outcome. Not because I read it daily — because when something odd happens (and "huh, why did it reschedule that?" does happen), the answer is a query away instead of a vibe. An unlogged agent action is unreviewable, and unreviewable autonomy is just hope with a scheduler.

The pattern generalises far beyond agents: allowlist, tiered consequence, audit. It's the same envelope banks put around payment operations and ops teams put around production access. LLMs didn't make it necessary; they made it necessary at home.

529 tests, none of them online

ALFRED's CI runs 529 tests fully offline — the model layer mocked, the tool layer real. That split is deliberate. What needs proving isn't whether the LLM writes a nice weekly plan (it varies; that's the nature of the beast). What needs proving is that no plan, however malformed, can escape the envelope: that a tool outside the allowlist is refused, that a tier violation is blocked, that every refusal is logged. Determinism belongs at the boundary. Test the cage, not the tiger.

The honest open question

The Brain keeps a note called Open Questions, and the first one is ALFRED's: how autonomous should tier 3 get? The uncomfortable finding after months of running it is that every increase in autonomy has been paid for with an increase in audit surface. Let the Conductor reschedule freely and you must now review schedules; let it message proactively and you must now review tone. The work doesn't disappear — it changes shape, from doing to supervising. Autonomy is not free labour; it's a trade where you swap execution for oversight, and the trade is only good when oversight is genuinely cheaper.

Which yields my only firm rule so far: expand autonomy one tier at a time, and only after the audit trail for the current tier has become boring. Boring is the signal. If reviewing the log still surprises you, the agent isn't ready for the next tier — and neither are you.

ALFRED is open source and in active development — the repo is here, envelope, tests and all.
